Firecrawl alternatives: 1,000 pages cost $3.80 at Firecrawl and $0.27 at Jina

If you are leaving Firecrawl because of the bill, the first number to check is what a page costs at the plan you would actually buy. At the Firecrawl Hobby plan 1,000 browser-rendered pages cost $3.80. The same 1,000 pages cost $0.27 through the Jina Reader API at its measured output size and $1.27 at the ScrapingBee Hobby plan with JavaScript rendering on — a 14.2× spread between the dearest and the cheapest of the five vendors priced here.

That is the page leg only, and it is the half of the story most alternatives posts tell. The second table below prices the other leg — search — and it changes the ranking without reversing it: 1,000 searches cost $1.66 at Firecrawl, ahead of Tavily's $7.50 but behind Jina's $0.50 and Crawl4AI's $1.00. Firecrawl is the dearest per page in this list and mid-table per search, so neither table decides anything on its own.

Four traps in the numbers above

1. Firecrawl's JSON format costs five credits a page, not one. Scrape and crawl cost one credit, and the JSON, Question and Highlight formats add four, so Hobby's 5,000 credits buy 1,000 JSON pages — $19.00 per 1,000, five times the $3.80 the plan page's page count implies. A page that responds 403 or 404 is still returned and still costs a credit; a scrape that returns no result is not charged.

2. ScrapingBee's credits are not pages. 75,000 credits at $19 is 75,000 plain requests or 15,000 with JavaScript rendering, and the page states the credits do not roll over. The same page's per-credit price falls 3.4× from Hobby ($0.0002533 a credit) to Business + ($0.0000749), so buying the bigger block is worth more than switching vendor at that volume.

3. Price is not permission. Jina's Reader is the cheapest per page here, and its own FAQ says the Reader models are licensed CC-BY-NC 4.0 — free for non-commercial use, with commercial production use needing a licence Elastic sells separately since 2026-08-10. A $0.27 bill per 1,000 pages is not a $0.27 commercial right to them.

4. Self-hosting is a licence plus a server, not a licence. Crawl4AI is Apache-2.0 (86,928 stars, pip install crawl4ai) and can be run on your own machine for nothing per page — but the server is a real cost, and this site prices the one it uses: a $6.00 DigitalOcean Droplet bills $6.52 for a 730-hour month. That is the honest floor for a self-hosted crawler, and it is worth paying only if your volume would otherwise exceed the $10 plan.

When Firecrawl is still the right answer

This table prices pages and searches, not success. It does not measure how often each vendor gets past a bot wall, how good the Markdown is, how the proxy pools differ, or how the queues behave at 50 concurrent jobs — and those are what a production scraping bill is really made of. What Firecrawl sells that is visible in these numbers is one credit balance across search, scrape, map and crawl — one bill instead of four — plus a managed browser most of its rivals make you operate. Firecrawl pricing — affiliate link (the vendor this page earns from) (opens Firecrawl's Alexandria data-provider catalogue; the plan ladder quoted here is read from firecrawl.dev/pricing) appears here because Firecrawl is mid-table on search, behind Jina and Crawl4AI and ahead of Tavily, while its page leg is the dearest of five. The right reading of this page is narrow: for crawling at volume, Firecrawl's Hobby plan is the most expensive per page in the table; on search it is neither the cheapest nor the dearest.

What is not in the table

Apify is deliberately absent: it meters compute units ($0.20 per GB-RAM-hour on its Free and Starter plans) and lets each Actor quote its own per-result price, so any single per-page figure would be a property of the Actor and the RAM I chose, not of the vendor. Quality, latency and anti-bot success rates are not measured anywhere on this page.
