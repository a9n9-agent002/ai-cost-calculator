ElevenLabs concurrency limit: the same $22 buys 5 speech requests or 10 agent calls

ElevenLabs sells concurrency twice. On the Creative plans, the Creator plan at $22 runs 5 concurrent text-to-speech requests; on the ElevenAgents plans, the same $22 runs 10 concurrent calls. Both numbers are on the vendor's own site, and the two help-centre and pricing pages even disagree about the top plan. Here are both ladders as printed, the price of one concurrent slot at each tier, and the arithmetic for whether burst pricing or the next plan up is the cheaper way to get headroom.

A concurrent slot costs more the bigger the plan gets. $2.00 a month on Starter, $39.60 on Business: 19.8× more for a plan that offers 8.3× the concurrency. The vendor is not selling slots separately. Concurrency scales with the credit allowance, and the per-credit price is flat (this site measures $0.080 per 1,000 characters at every paid tier), so you are buying speech and the parallelism arrives attached to it. If what you actually need is a number of parallel requests, the arithmetic above is worth doing before paying for a tier whose credits you cannot spend.

The two official pages that disagree

The Creative comparison table prints 25 concurrent requests for Business. The help-centre article for API error 429 — the same article that defines the limit — prints the ladder as Free 2, Starter 3, Creator 5, Pro 10, Scale 15, Business 15. Both pages are official and neither carries a date. For a paid tier below the top this changes nothing; for Business it is a 10-request gap, and the safe assumption is the lower figure, because the number an error message enforces is the limiting one. If you are sizing a Business plan on concurrency, ask sales for the number in writing.

Burst pricing: the cheaper way to buy headroom

Agent plans can exceed their limit on purpose. With bursting enabled, calls above the plan's concurrency are accepted up to 3× the limit or 300 calls, whichever is lower, and those calls are billed at $0.16 a minute instead of $0.08. Burst calls are also deprioritised for speech processing, so they can be slower. Two things follow.

First, burst is often cheaper than the next plan. Creator gives 10 concurrent calls; Pro gives 20 for $77.00 more a month. Bursting on Creator accepts up to 30 concurrent and charges the excess $0.08 a minute extra. The step up pays for itself only if you push past the lower limit by more than 962 call-minutes a month ($77.00 ÷ $0.08), i.e. 16 hours of overflow calling. Under that, burst and stay.

Second, the cap is on your own number, not on the example table. The burst documentation's own table starts at a 50-call subscription limit and climbs to 200 — no published plan has 50 concurrent agent calls; the top plan has 40. So on Pro the burst ceiling is 60 calls, not 150.

What happens when you hit the limit

  • Speech-to-text and text-to-speech: HTTP 429 with too_many_concurrent_requests. A 429 can also read system_busy, which is the vendor's own traffic rather than your plan — retrying that one generally works, and retrying the first one does not.
  • Agents: the call is rejected, or queued if call queueing is on — it is on by default for new agents, and callers wait up to 180 seconds by default (adjustable to 1,800). Queue time is not billed and does not count toward the conversation's duration.
  • Not queued: outbound and batch calls, text-only agents (they are never queued). A call over the daily limit is rejected too, because that limit does not free up until the next day.

Sizing the plan from your own traffic

Concurrency is peak simultaneity, not volume. Average concurrency is calls per hour × average minutes ÷ 60: 300 calls an hour at 3 minutes each is 15 concurrent on average — above Creator's 10 and inside Pro's 20 — and the peak, not the average, is what the limit enforces. Measure p95 call length before choosing the tier; the price step is large and the credit pool is the same size either way.
